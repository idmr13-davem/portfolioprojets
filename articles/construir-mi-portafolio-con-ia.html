Building my portfolio with AI: what I decided and learned to delegate

November 2026 · English · 8 min read

My experience integrating AI, from content analysis to building and publishing my portfolio.

  • Artificial intelligence
  • Web design

If you have ever had to create a portfolio or a website for your business, you have probably faced questions like these: What should I show? What do I need to say, and how should I tell the story? What do I want someone to understand about me or my business when they arrive?

For me, designing my own portfolio is one of the most challenging tasks in my profession. It means looking at my work, choosing what to show, and finding a way to explain it to people who arrive with different expectations.

And, to top it all off, how does the arrival of AI change this challenge?

Over the past month, I decided to create a new version of my portfolio. To make it more interesting, I wanted to work differently from how I had built previous versions: involving AI from the analysis stage through to building and publishing the site.

Today, I want to share what the process was like and what I learned along the way.

1. Who I am designing for

As a UX designer, starting by understanding the people who will use a product is part of my job. But this time, I wondered what AI could contribute at that early stage and how I could use it to challenge my own ideas.

With its help, I outlined three potential visitor profiles using proto-personas. I used them as hypotheses to organize the content, considering what they might need and how much time they would be willing to spend on it.

  • Recruiters: people who find my profile and need to quickly understand whether my experience fits an open role. I could not expect them to explore my entire website or read a full case study just to discover my role, my background, or the kinds of projects I have worked on.
  • Design leads: people interested in understanding my decisions, my involvement in projects, the results, and the quality of the visual execution. To do so, they would be willing to explore a portfolio in greater depth.
  • Potential clients and business stakeholders: people who want to know how I could help with their challenges and how my work relates to product or business outcomes. They look for evidence of impact over execution and craft.

Although these profiles are not completely fixed, and the same person may have more than one interest, thinking about these priorities helped me ask a more useful question: what does each person need to find to decide whether they want to learn more about my work?

2. What to share, and when

These profiles gave me an initial sense of how to organize the content, both across the site as a whole and within each case study.

At this stage, I used AI to explore references and compare portfolios. Then I began looking at what each one said about the person behind it and which aspects of their work they chose to highlight.

I found portfolios where visual quality took center stage: interfaces, interactions, and details that turned something everyday into an extraordinary experience. Others devoted more space to explaining the problem, the decisions, and their connection to business metrics. And in some, especially those focused on animation, the site itself served as a demonstration of what its creator could do.

After exploring them, I asked myself: who am I, and what do I want someone to recognize in my work? That helped me define which aspects I wanted to highlight and how to organize the information so each visitor could find what they were looking for.

The question was no longer just which projects to include. I also needed to decide what someone should understand before opening them, at first glance, and as the case study provided more context.

3. Shaping the content and testing it

Having an initial understanding of my visitors, their potential needs, and how I wanted to present myself allowed me to create a first “content sketch.”

Before getting into visual design, I worked on the narrative: what to say about myself, how to present the projects, and how much context each story needed to make sense. This part is especially challenging for those of us who are not content writers or come from a UX writing background. Some of the questions I had were: How much should I explain? How much content should I include? And at what point is it too much?

I used AI simulations and built custom content skills for my workflow. I followed a writing-and-iteration process and used the three profiles I had outlined (recruiter, design lead, and project manager) as references in a synthetic user test to explore questions and identify potential gaps. I also received feedback from real people throughout the process, which helped me review what I was building from other perspectives.

This was where I began to see the value of integrating AI from the start. The context from the previous steps helped me better direct the tasks I assigned to AI and maintain a connection between the analysis and the content.

Even so, reviewing the suggestions was still part of my job. I had to decide what added to the story, what was unnecessary, and what did not accurately reflect my experience.

4. Designing, building, and publishing

In total, my portfolio went through three design versions. With each one, I delegated a little more of the execution as I came to understand the results I could achieve and how to better guide the work.

At first, I was deeply involved in every visual detail. I wanted control over the final finish, which meant the early versions took days or even weeks.

The final version, by contrast, took less than a week of work. By then, I had a clearer sense of what I needed to design myself and what I could delegate with specific instructions and references.

Working with Claude Code and Codex also led me to get involved in building the site, learn to work with Git and GitHub, and move forward with each new version until I saw it published on my own domain.

That brought up another question: deciding when it was ready to launch. At first, I did not want to publish anything I was not 100% happy with. I worried about the impression the first visitors would take away. But waiting also meant continuing to make tiny adjustments based on my own assumptions. Having the portfolio live allowed me to get fresh perspectives and spot things I had stopped noticing.

Publishing became part of the process, not its endpoint. I could review, correct, and keep improving with each new version.

What I learned from working with AI

This project taught me several lessons that I am already applying to other work. Beyond which skill, agent, LLM, or framework to use, what interested me most was understanding how to integrate AI into the way I design and build.

Providing context is part of the work, too

I have seen many tools promise to create an entire ecosystem from a single initial instruction. Whenever I see that, I wonder: who decides which image, text, or composition to use? And beyond that, what about the “intangible” decisions that are not immediately visible, such as: who am I speaking to? And why am I saying this instead of something else? Using AI does not mean handing over decisions about what you, as the owner of the final product, want to communicate to whoever visits your portfolio or website. Asking AI for something is quick and easy. Explaining what you want to achieve, who you are working for, and which constraints it must respect takes more dedication.

In my experience, that effort helped connect the tasks to one another. I could revisit earlier decisions, use them as references, and assess whether what I was building still made sense.

Speed helps when you have a direction

The difference between the early versions and the final one was very clear to me. As I clarified the content, visual direction, and sequence of work, I was able to move faster. AI helped me explore and implement changes, while I focused on deciding which changes to keep and what needed improvement.

I also learned to recognize when I was spending too much time on a task I could delegate and then review.

What to decide and what to delegate

One question helped me a great deal throughout the process:

What do I need to decide myself, and what can AI do with the decision I have already made?

In my case, I wanted to remain responsible for the message, the site’s priorities, the visual direction, and reviewing the result. That allowed me to let go of some of the execution with greater confidence. I had a basis for evaluating proposals and deciding when something needed another attempt.

Publishing helps you learn, too

Seeing the site working and receiving feedback gave me insights I had not found while refining it on my own.

There are still things I want to improve, but I now have a published version I can keep working on. For me, that shift mattered: moving from waiting for the ideal moment to having something real that I can review and iterate on.

From my portfolio to other projects

Creating my portfolio allowed me to go all the way from the first questions about analysis and building context to a published site with a direction of its own, with AI supporting the process from start to finish.

It also helped me better define how I want to work: understanding what a project needs to communicate, making decisions with clear intent, and using AI to speed up the build without ever stopping my review of the result.
